In one of the burial holes cut into the wall, bones moved.

The Keeper had come fewer than a hundred steps down the stairs.

A rusted helmet rolled to the floor. A hand of bone pressed against the ground.

Wherever the light reached, the burial holes rattled.

Three. Five.

Skeletons gripping dull spears rose toward the light. Empty eye sockets turned this way all at once.

They were not like the ones in the village. They wore armor. They kept their ranks.

Soldiers.

From the far side of the dark came the sound of a bowstring drawn.

The Keeper dropped low. An arrow skimmed over the lantern and snapped against the wall behind.

The skeletons were fast but clumsy. All their bodies remembered was drill from long ago.

The Keeper slipped past a spearpoint. Cut the cord with the dagger. The bones spilled out, helmet and all.

The next one they struck with the scythe. A scythe given to them for cutting the roots that block the way.

Each time one fell, a small, dim flame floated up.

The flames from the village had been clear. These were different. Crumbs that had been in the dark so long they had lost the shape of a person.

Still, with each one that came in, the light widened by a hand's breadth.

*That light isn't yours. It's borrowed. You give it back at the Door.*

The teacher's words.

The one shooting arrows stood on a heap of stones.

When the Keeper drew near, it threw down its bow and charged bare-handed.

The moment the cord was cut, something glinted on the crumbling wrist. An iron name tag, its letters worn away.

A knife rolled out from between the ribs.

Its blade was only two finger joints long. Too small to be a soldier's knife.

The Keeper looked at it once and moved on.

---

In a wide room at the end of the corridor stood three stone braziers.

All three were out.

*To carry a great soul, light the braziers along the way first. Take it down a dark road, and it will lose its way.*

They had only been taught it. This was the first time they had seen one.

The Keeper moved the lantern's fire to the small lamp. Touched it to the hardened oil in the brazier's bowl.

The fire caught and faltered. They touched it again. It sank again.

Outside the room, the scrape of bone on stone came closer.

They did not put down the lamp.

A low, red fire rose in the first brazier.

As they lit the second, skeletons poured in. The Keeper fought with their back to the brazier.

When the third caught, the only one left standing in the room was the Keeper.

From the stone door on the far side came the sound of something coming loose.

---

In a narrow room beside the stone door stood a stone altar.

A place where Lantern Keepers had set down their lanterns and rested. Around the round hollow, layers of oil stains had soaked in.

Setting the lantern in the hollow, the Keeper's hand stopped.

On the edge of the altar, three short lines in soot.

They touched them. The soot came off on their fingertip.

The soot on the doorpost had soaked into the grain and hardened. This had not hardened yet.

It could not be three hundred years old. A few years.

The teacher.

The teacher who went down a few years ago had come this far. Had rested here. And then gone further down.

*The third is the hardest.*

Only now did those words sound a little different.

When you put out a flame at the Door, that soul crosses over. Once it crosses, you can never see it again.

The day the teacher drew three lines on the doorpost, they had said that and smiled. Why putting it out was the hardest, the Keeper had not asked then.

They had thought they would come to know someday. The teacher would be there beside them.

A name circled in their mouth.

The Keeper closed their lips.

Then they heard a voice.

They turned. No one was there.

From a crack in the wall where the lantern light fell, a low, tired man's voice was seeping out.

> The day His Majesty closed his eyes, a fire was lit in every house in the kingdom.
> The Master of the Lantern Keepers came, bringing one young apprentice.

In the light, an ember sparkled and vanished.

An Echo.

A voice the dead had dropped along the road. They said you could only hear it when lantern light reached it. This was the first time the Keeper had heard one.

The kingdom. The land where the villagers' ancestors had lived, gone three hundred years.

The Keeper heard the same voice once more, at the end of a dead-end corridor.

> The procession went down first, to light the braziers along the road.
> The Master lit a vigil fire before the tomb and said,
> "We will return for him."

The Keeper stood there a long while.

Three hundred years ago, a man called the Master had said that and gone down.

And had not come back.

A few years ago, the teacher too had gone down this road, leaving word of coming back.

*When the road opens, I'll come back.*

That was as much as the Keeper heard.

The voice remembered far more.

## Those Who Kept the Fire

We remember that day.

Three hundred years ago. When we were still alive.

The day His Majesty closed his eyes, a fire was lit in every house in the kingdom.

We watched it from the top of the walls.

As the sun went down, one by one, starting from the lower town. Under the eaves, at the windows, in the doorways.

The vigil fire, lit in a house where someone has died.

That day it was lit even in houses where no one had died. Not one house went without.

It was as if the whole land had raised a lantern.

One of us wept.

We don't know who. We were all weeping.

---

The Master of the Lantern Keepers came, bringing one young apprentice.

The Lantern Keepers who kept the kingdom's Funeral Road.

Gray cloaks lined up in the palace courtyard.

Usually one or two came to take the dead away. That day the whole order came.

"A great soul cannot be carried alone."

Someone told us that.

The Master was a tall man of few words.

When our captain asked him something, he nodded once or shook his head. That was all.

We never once saw that man smile.

There was a child beside him.

Twelve, maybe thirteen. Wearing the same gray cloak as the Master, the hem dragging on the ground.

---

The procession could not leave at once.

They said that to carry a great soul, the braziers along the way must be lit first. Take it down a dark road, and it will lose its way.

So the procession would go down first. Light every brazier, then come back up and take His Majesty.

We laid His Majesty in the tomb beneath the palace. Where the Funeral Road begins.

The Master went to the stone stand in front of the stone coffin.

He moved the fire of his lantern to a lamp. Lit the oil bowl on the stand with it.

The fire caught quietly.

The Master looked down at that fire a long time. Then turned to us.

"We will return for him."

It was the longest thing we ever heard that man say.

---

Before leaving, the child came to us.

The child stood in front of the youngest of us. A boy who had received his spear that year.

The two were only a few years apart.

The child took a small knife from their belt. Then crouched in front of the vigil fire.

And trimmed the very tip of the wick standing in the middle of the oil bowl.

The fire dipped for a moment. Then rose straighter than before.

"See?"

The boy nodded.

"If the fire gets drowsy, trim the wick a little. Then it'll stand up again."

The child pressed the knife into the boy's hand.

He didn't know what to do. The child smiled.

"I'll be right back."

The child ran to the Master, cloak dragging.

The Master bent without a word. Gathered up the hem of the child's cloak and tucked it into the belt. So it wouldn't drag on the ground.

The procession went down first, to light the braziers along the road. Down the stairs behind the tomb.

The lanterns grew small in the dark, one by one.

The last one we could see was the smallest lantern.

---

We were given one order. Guard this fire.

And one charge.

Do not speak His Majesty's name until he has crossed.

They said if you call a name, the soul looks back. And if it looks back, it cannot leave.

We had received our spears under that name. Fought shouting that name.

So we were all the more careful. Drunk, or in our dreams, we said only 'His Majesty.'

Guarding the fire was not hard.

Filling the oil, shielding it from wind, trimming the wick. We stood in shifts.

The boy trimmed the wick. With that knife. He left it to no one else.

Ten days. Nothing to report.

A month. Nothing to report.

---

No one came back.

After a year, our captain sent two down.

They went with torches. We were not Lantern Keepers.

A torch doesn't show the things down below. We didn't know that then.

Those two never came back either.

Our captain never sent anyone down again.

Word came from above.

That the Queen had not crowned a new king. That she would do it when word came that His Majesty had crossed.

No word came.

Fewer came to relieve us. Then none.

The oil sent down from the palace stopped too.

We poured in the oil from our own lamps. When that ran out, we searched abandoned houses.

Every year there were more abandoned houses.

We grew old, and one by one we were buried at His Majesty's side.

Those who died first were buried by those who remained.

We were not laid down. We were buried kneeling, facing His Majesty. At our post, in the posture of guarding.

When the space beside His Majesty was full, we dug holes in the corridor walls for the rest.

The boy lived the longest.

In the last few years he searched for oil alone, trimmed the wick alone.

There was no one left to bury him.

He knelt beside the stone coffin himself. And did not rise again.

The knife held to his chest.

Even buried, we guarded the fire.

We could guard it without bodies.

We were beside the fire. The fire held on to us.

It wasn't cold.

Waiting is something you can do without a body.

---

The day the fire went out, His Majesty rose.

There was no wind.

The oil the boy had last poured was still about half full in the bowl.

And yet the fire leaned.

Toward the stairs.

As if someone below drew a breath, the flame stretched long and fell toward the stairs.

And went out.

For the first time, we were cold.

For the first time, we were afraid.

Who we had been, why we were here, grew faint.

Only one thing remained.

We must guard.

Without even knowing what.

On the stone coffin, something sat up.

It was His Majesty.

His Majesty called us.

We pushed the earth aside, gripped our spears, and stood.

So it was in life. So it was in death.

The boy went toward the stairs. Picking up a dead friend's bow.

To be the first to see whoever came.

After that, it was dark for a long time.

---

Long after, the Queen passed by here on her way down.

A single light footstep came down the stairs.

She passed the tomb chamber without even slowing. In the dark, she could not have seen.

His Majesty raised his head.

As if he had heard something.

Then lowered it again.

His Majesty did not know her.

We knew her.

That light footstep, we had heard countless times on the walls.

But we could not speak. We were forgetting what there was to say.

A little later, torches came down.

The clink of armor. The same sound as ours.

We stood. When we saw fire, we had to.

Only after the fires went out did we learn who they were.

Those who had served the Queen.

They had died and risen, and followed her further down.

They did not look back at us.

---

Very long after, a light came down the stairs.

One lantern. One set of footsteps.

We stood. All that was left in us was that we must rise when we saw a light.

One of us, from deep within a very faded memory, saw that light and thought,

*You came back.*

We don't know if it was the boy.

The name tag still hung on that wrist. The small knife was still at that chest.

He threw down the bow and ran bare-handed toward the light.

The light cut him down.

He felt no pain.

He looked warm.

That light was now coming toward His Majesty's chamber.

---

## The King's Tomb

The tomb at the end of the road was an entire room.

In the middle lay a stone coffin.

Around it, dozens of skeletons lay half covered in earth. Kneeling, foreheads to the floor. Facing the stone coffin.

In front of the coffin was a stone stand for holding a fire.

An empty oil bowl sat on it.

On the ceiling above, soot had spread wide. A place where a fire had burned for a very long time.

Nothing lay inside the stone coffin.

Something sat on top of it.

A head taller than a man. The hem of a robe that must once have been red. A crown untouched by rust.

It raised its head.

"You who carry the light. Leave my tomb."

The voice echoed through the whole room.

The Keeper did not leave.

"You said you would return."

The king stood.

"You said to light the fire and wait. I waited. My soldiers waited. Until they grew old and died and were buried at my side. Even buried."

The heads that had been bowed rose one by one.

"And then the fire went out. You did not come."

The soldiers stood.

---

The king was not clumsy.

He struck the floor with the tip of his sword. At the sound, another soldier buried around him raised its head.

As long as the king called, the soldiers rose. For three hundred years it had been so.

Spears came in from every side.

The Keeper struck them down, stepped aside, struck them down again.

Each time a flame came in, the lantern grew brighter. The brighter it grew, the more of the space between the king's ribs could be seen.

A large, dim fire.

From it, dozens of thin threads stretched out. To each soldier in the room.

Only then did the Keeper understand.

It was not the king holding on to his soldiers.

It was the soldiers holding on to the king.

Their hands stopped.

The king's sword did not miss the opening.

The Keeper rolled across the floor. The lantern slipped from their hand.

A hand's breadth before it hit the stone, they snatched it back. The fire inside the glass reeled.

It reeled.

It did not go out.

*Never let the fire go out.*

The Keeper stood and cut those threads.

Each time the scythe passed, a soldier fell to its knees. And did not rise again.

When the last thread was cut, the king stood leaning on his sword.

Alone.

The cord that held the king to his body was thick.

The dagger slipped once. Twice.

On the third try, it parted.

---

The crown fell first, ringing clear.

The bones crumbled, and the red cloth settled slowly down.

A large, dim flame floated up. Like eyes after a long cry.

The flame did not come to the lantern.

It was waiting.

The Keeper opened the lantern's little door and knelt. At the same height as the soldiers buried bowing.

"We're late."

Nothing else came to mind.

"I will take you to the Door."

The flame came slowly and settled beside the Keeper's fire.

The light reached the far end of the room.

From the fallen soldiers, flames floated up one by one. They followed the king into the lantern.

As if a procession were following behind.

The Keeper looked back once at the empty oil bowl. Then stood.

The lantern weighed the same. Even so, it was heavy.

A funeral three hundred years late. A funeral that would only end at the Door.

Behind the tomb chamber were low stairs. A damp smell of earth came up. The smell of tree roots.

Inside the lantern, the king's flame leaned toward those stairs.

Because that was where the Door was, no doubt.

That is what the Keeper thought.
