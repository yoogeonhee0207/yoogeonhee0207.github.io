The sun hung on the ridge.

The Lantern Keeper ran down the slope. Each time the lantern swung, the fire inside the glass swung with it.

They had to get there before sundown.

When someone dies, the fire is lit before that day's sun goes down. Even the children in the village knew it. Too late, and the soul is left alone in the dark.

The third house by the well. People had gathered in the yard.

When the Keeper stepped through the brushwood gate, the talking stopped.

The people drew back to either side and made a path. No one met their eyes. They only bowed their heads.

There was no hatred in it. The Keeper was someone who touched death, and so people kept their distance.

The Keeper knew it too. So they did not step up onto the porch, but stood at the threshold.

"You're not too late."

The child's mother pointed under the eaves. An empty oil bowl hung there. The wick was new.

"If you couldn't come, we were going to borrow the neighbors' fire."

"That would be fine. A fire passed from fire to fire is all the same fire."

The Keeper took a small lamp from their belt.

They opened the lantern's little door and touched the lamp's wick to the flame. The fire caught. They tilted the lamp into the bowl under the eaves.

A small fire stood up.

At first it was no different from any other fire.

Then the flame swelled once, as if drawing a breath. As if someone had walked out from inside the house and sat down there. Its color grew a little warmer.

Someone in the yard began to sob.

"That's it now. Check the oil morning and evening, and shield it on windy days."

It was a vigil fire. The fire that holds a soul until a Lantern Keeper comes to take it.

As the Keeper turned to go, someone caught the hem of their cloak.

A child. Six or seven, maybe.

The grown-ups caught their breath. The mother hurried to pull the child's hand away.

The Keeper shook their head. Then crouched down to the child's eye level.

"Is Grandma in there?"

"Yes. She's holding on to that fire."

"There's fire in the kitchen too. Why can't it be the kitchen fire?"

The day they first received their lantern, the Keeper had asked their teacher the very same thing.

"Because the kitchen fire is only fire. A fire that burns nothing but oil and wood has nowhere for a soul to hold on to."

The Keeper lifted the lantern to show.

"There's a piece of my soul inside this. I took it out and put it in on the day I became a Lantern Keeper. Only a fire with a living soul in it can hold the soul of the dead. Grandma's fire was lit from this one, so it's the same fire."

The child peered into the glass.

"Doesn't it hurt?"

"……A little."

The child watched the fire for a long time. Then asked again.

"When is Grandma going?"

The yard went quiet.

"By rights, I'm supposed to take her. Carry her in this lantern, down there."

The Keeper pointed to the middle of the village. There was a stone gate. Inside it, stairs went down beneath the earth.

"That road is called the Funeral Road. If you go all the way down, there's a door. The Door to the Far Side. When I put out her flame in front of that door, Grandma crosses over."

"Then go now."

"I can't. The road is blocked. It has been for three hundred years."

"Who blocked it?"

"No one knows. None of the Lantern Keepers who went down ever came back."

The child looked up at the eaves.

Beside Grandma's bowl hung oil bowls in a row. Grandpa's fire. His grandpa's fire above it. The fires of ancestors no one remembered the faces of.

Every house in the village was the same. Three hundred years of fires, burning, and not one of them able to cross.

The child opened their mouth.

"My grandma's name is—"

The mother's hand covered the child's mouth. The whole yard froze.

The Keeper spoke quietly.

"Until she has crossed, you mustn't call her name."

"……Why?"

"Because if you call it, she'll look back. And if she looks back, she can't leave."

"What about calling her Grandma?"

"That's all right."

The Keeper stood up.

"Just don't let it go out. As long as that fire burns, Grandma is here."

The child nodded.

That was last spring.

---

The Lantern Keeper lived alone on the ridge at the edge of the village.

On the slope beside the path up, there was a hollow sealed with stones. No one knew who had sealed it or when. Even the children did not play in front of it.

On the doorpost of the house were three short lines drawn in soot.

The teacher had drawn them. The three vows of a Lantern Keeper.

Never let the fire go out.

Take every soul you carry all the way to the Door.

At the Door, put out the flame, whoever's it is.

*The third is the hardest.*

The day the teacher drew the three lines on the doorpost, that is what they said, and smiled. When the teacher smiled, their head tilted a little to one side.

The teacher never said what it meant.

It was the teacher who had passed an ember into the Keeper's empty lantern. *I'll give you the ember. The soul you add yourself.* From that day the fire was the Keeper's own. If it went out, they would lose their way.

The teacher had a teacher too. And above that one, another. The village had always had one Lantern Keeper, generation after generation.

And all of them did the same thing. They handed their fire to an apprentice, then went down to open the road.

None of them came back.

A few years ago, the teacher went down too.

Out through this doorway, to the stone gate in the middle of the village. Down the stairs inside. Under the Funeral Road.

They left two sayings behind.

*When the road opens, I'll come back.*

*If I don't, come down and send me off.*

The Keeper watched the teacher's back from in front of the stone gate.

There was a mended patch on the left shoulder of the purple cloak. The Keeper had sewn it the year they first held a needle, so the stitches were crooked. The teacher never had it sewn again.

The lantern grew small down the stairs. The Keeper watched until the light was a single point, and then gone.

The teacher did not come back.

Since then the Keeper had never once called the teacher's name. The teacher could not have crossed yet.

So the Keeper lit fires alone. A new fire in the houses of the dead. Oil every evening for the old graves on the hill whose families had died out.

Never sending, only holding. As the ones before had done for three hundred years.

They thought it would go on like that.

---

The first one went out at the end of summer.

The old grave at the very end of the hill. There was oil left. There was no wind. Only the fire was gone.

The earth of the grave had been dug open from the inside.

A fire that goes out because the oil runs dry is all right. The soul lingers a while beside the cold wick. Light it again, and the soul comes back.

A fire that goes out because something pulled at it is different. The soul is torn away along with the fire. A soul that has lost the fire it held on to twists, takes hold of its own body, and rises.

Light a new fire, and it does not come back.

One a month. By autumn, one every two days.

And then that night.

The fires on the hill went out one by one.

As if someone were pinching them out with a finger. In order, from the end. One. One. Another.

The Keeper did not count to ten. They grabbed the lantern and kicked the door open.

---

On the road below the hill, an old man sat slumped on the ground. The old man from the house by the well.

A broken paper lantern rolled beside him.

He was staring into the dark. There was nothing there.

"Something, something grabbed my leg……"

The Keeper stood in front of him and raised the lantern.

When the light touched them, they were there.

Six.

Not ten steps away. Bent bodies covered in earth, crawling, staggering closer.

A torch doesn't show them. Only a light with a soul in it can light up a soul.

The moment the light touched them, they saw the Keeper too. Six heads turned this way at once.

What they hungered for was not people. It was fire. The place they had lost, the place they once held on to.

"Go home!"

The old man fled, half crawling. The one in front lunged.

The dagger went first.

A thin blade the length of a palm cut the cord that ties body to soul. The bent body crumbled into earth.

From that spot a small flame floated up. It was drawn into the lantern. Once inside, it stopped struggling and grew still.

Two. Three.

Nails tore the purple cloak.

Four. Five.

When the Keeper turned, the sixth was gone.

*Don't chase them out of the light.*

The teacher's voice.

*Out there, you can't see them, and they can see you.*

The Keeper held their breath.

In the dark, there was a sound of earth being scraped. Beyond the wall.

They turned slowly. The edge of the light swept across the wall, passed over it, and reached the doorway of the house beyond.

The door was open.

A child stood there.

Cupping a small oil bowl in both hands. The fire that should have been hanging under the eaves. The child had taken it down and was holding it close, as if shielding it from the wind.

The sixth was right in front of the child.

Drawn by that little light, it was reaching toward the child's face.

The Keeper went over the wall. The body moved before the thought.

Rolling in between the child and the thing, they thrust out the lantern. Hit full in the face by the light, it faltered.

That one beat was enough.

The sound of earth falling apart. A flame came into the lantern.

The child did not cry. Only the fingers wrapped around the bowl had gone white.

The fire in the bowl burned small, but straight.

"You held on to it well."

"You told me not to let it go out."

The child's voice shook. Their eyes went to the heap of earth.

"If it goes out, Grandma turns into that too."

The Keeper could not find an answer.

The child was right.

"Go inside. Shut the door. Hold on to the fire."

---

Eleven fires went out that night. Eleven rose.

The last one did not come toward the village.

It was heading for the stone gate. Toward the stairs that go down beneath the earth, crawling. As if something were calling it.

The Keeper cut it down in front of the stone gate.

Then looked down at the lantern.

Tonight's eleven were wavering inside the glass.

All of them were pressed flat against one side of the glass.

The side facing the stone gate.

The Keeper held the lantern out toward the stairs.

Then the fire in the very middle, the one that must never go out, stretched long toward the stairs.

As if someone below were drawing in a breath.

The Keeper pulled the lantern back, almost hugging it. The fire stood again.

Their hands shook. Hands that had not shaken while fighting.

It was down there. The thing that put fires out.

The thing that had blocked the road for three hundred years was now reaching all the way up.

One a month. One every two days. Tonight, eleven at once.

The village held three hundred years' worth of fires.

They could not be held any longer.

At this rate, every fire in the village would go out before winter.

Wherever a fire went out, the dead would rise. One person could not cut them all down.

There was only one way to stop it.

To go down.

To go down and stop the thing that put the fires out. To open the blocked road.

Once the road was open, the souls could be taken to the Door. They could be sent off. And once they were sent off, there would be nothing left to rise.

It was what the teacher had meant to do. What the teacher's teacher had meant to do.

The ones before had handed their fire to an apprentice and gone down.

The Keeper had no apprentice.

If they did not come back, there would be no Lantern Keeper left in the village.

Still, they had to go down. If they waited, the fires they were guarding would be gone first.

---

At dawn the Keeper stood before the shelves in the house on the ridge.

Small oil bowls burned in rows. The souls of those who had risen this autumn.

They could not go back to the fires of their own graves. So the Keeper had lit new fires and kept them here. Before they knew it, three shelves were full.

They lit one fire in each of eleven empty bowls.

When they opened the lantern's little door, tonight's flames slipped out one by one and moved into the new fires. The Keeper waited until the last one had settled.

If you go down carrying souls and do not come back, you lose the souls you carried too.

That was why the ones before had also gone down with empty lanterns.

Now only the Keeper's own fire was left inside the lantern.

The weight could not have changed. Even so, it felt light. So light their hand felt empty.

They packed. The dagger. The scythe. The small lamp and oil. Water and bread. And the lantern.

Going out the door, they stopped in front of the doorpost.

Three soot lines.

*If I don't, come down and send me off.*

A name circled in their mouth.

The Keeper closed their lips.

---

The village was awake.

Someone stood in the doorway of every house.

No one came out into the road. No one spoke.

Everyone held a fire. Their ancestors' fires taken down from under the eaves, in both hands, so they would not go out.

The Keeper walked between them.

The newest of those fires had been lit by the teacher. The ones before by the teacher's teacher, and the ones before that by those who came before. Fires shared out from a single ember over three hundred years.

Those fires were raised now, one by one, along the road the Keeper walked.

Beyond the wall, in the doorway of that house, stood the child.

As the Keeper passed, the child lifted the oil bowl a little.

The child's lips moved without a sound.

*I didn't let it go out.*

The Keeper nodded once.

In front of the stone gate, they raised the lantern.

The stairs the teacher had gone down a few years ago. And before that, the teacher's teacher.

About ten steps were visible before the stairs sank into darkness. A cold, damp wind came up.

The fire in the lantern was pulled downward. Harder than before.

And at that moment.

In the darkness at the bottom of the stairs, very far away, something flickered once.

It was amber.

Like lantern light.

It could not be lantern light. In three hundred years, not one lantern had come back up from down there.

When they blinked, it was gone.

The Keeper gripped the lantern tight. The fire stood again.

The last Lantern Keeper took up a single lantern and descended the stairs.

Only where the light reaches is safe.
